# Pompeii: a short guided exploration

**Teacher reference · prototype · suggested 15–20 minutes.** Adapt to your group;
this is an optional discussion activity, not a curriculum-aligned or evaluated
course. Aelia, the other game characters, delivery dispute, prices and numerical
rules are fictional. The neighborhood is a simplified reconstruction.

**Possible objectives.** Learners may practice identifying a represented place
of work, explaining how an object or inscription supports a limited claim,
distinguishing an enslaved person from someone who had been freed, and separating
historical evidence from reconstruction and invented gameplay. Completion does
not establish that these objectives were learned.

**Before starting.** Use an agreed fresh life or separate practice copy. Preserve
existing saved work. Check the displayed time/pause notice and available controls;
ordinary movement and accepted work retain their game time costs. Leave optional
usage collection off unless explicitly agreed. Explain that slavery involved
coercion and unequal status; a paid shift is a different situation. Permit learners
to skip a card or stop the activity.

**Explore and discuss.**

1. Start with guidance. Ask the learner who Aelia is and what the immediate goal
   appears to be. Let them find a person, hear an account and choose whether to
   help. Review any cost or later obligation before confirmation; the teacher
   need not prescribe the most profitable choice.
2. Open an available history card. The food-counter and fullery cards concern
   archaeological features interpreted in the [Park's street account][streets].
   Compare one represented feature with the card's evidence and limits. A game
   model is not a measured ancient building.
3. Contrast Aelia with the commemorative example in the
   [Naevoleia Tyche source][tyche]. Her monument offers one particular case;
   it does not show how every freedwoman lived. If available, the wall-notice
   card provides a different evidence type: [museum-described painted
   inscriptions][elections]. A recommendation does not prove who won.
4. Read the actual result of the learner's choice. Distinguish an offer, an
   accepted arrangement, a later obligation and a completed effect. Reopen help
   when useful; no correct answer is required to continue the saved life.

**Optional ungraded reflection.**

- What feature suggests that a represented place served customers or processed
  cloth? Which linked source supports your explanation, and what remains uncertain?
- What does “freedwoman” mean? Why should one prominent person or fictional
  character not stand for every woman's life? The [Park's exhibition account][women]
  stresses varied social positions.
- Name one supported historical feature, one reconstruction and one invented
  choice. What actually changed after your own action?

Keep reflections oral or on a teacher-chosen local sheet; do not put learner
answers into NPC memory or usage events. Record any coaching if observing first-use
comprehension. A facilitated lesson is not an unassisted usability test. No source
photographs are reproduced here, and links do not grant image reuse rights.
